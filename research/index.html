Characteristics of feature subspaces

Most models start with the idea that an item, like a word, can be thought of as an ordered set of features and their values — a vector. The angle between the items in some very high-dimensional feature space tells you how similar they are (related to their dot product). These mathematical elements are a half a century old, originated by psychologists and are now at the core of large language models and many other AI models. But these models are small. We dive into the math to develop our intuition about how various processes actually could function to drive memory behaviour. Our attentional subsetting theory makes the models more realistic, and in doing so, is starting to capture new aspects of memory behaviour. We assume that of the very large number of potential features one could consider for an item such as a word, in a typical episodic memory task, people only work with a subset, a handful of such features. PENGUIN may evoke features like flightless, black and white colouring and fish-eater. Seeing PENGUIN again will likely evoke most of those same features — but different features than, say, HUMMINGBIRD. The working feature subset is low-dimensional and item-specific, but will typically reiterate. This gives episodic memory both a limberness and precision by making item representations effectively very distinctive from one another. Within this framework, the dimensionality of the active feature subspace (such as the spelling or phonological sounds of the word versus features related to the word’s meaning or imagining the word) can explain patterns of memory behaviour. We have explored this theory within item recognition and are now expanding it to memory for associations.

Linking brain activity to memory

Our approach is model-driven. However, cognitive neuroscience is still trying to identify what brain activity is relevant to understanding the brain basis of memory. We develop and refine approaches at a meta-level to bring us closer to identifying, and eventually understanding, that memory-relevant brain activity. We have done this by finding brain activity that differentiates later remembered from forgotten words (adopting the widely used subsequent memory effect and retrieval-success “trial-sorting” methods). But we further found memory-related brain activity while studying a word that covaries with different memory-related activity when tested on the word, completing the trajectory from encoding to retrieval. We also use classifiers inspired by machine learning ideas to identify activity that is formally predictive, not merely descriptive, of that later memory success. Finally, we are taking on mass univariate methods to be able to discover new spatiotemporal signatures of brain activity without the pitfalls of confirmation bias on the one hand, and multiple comparisons on the other.

Serial-order memory

Some models assume participants learn an ordered list (for example, 6 to 10 words, letters or digits) by linking each list-item to another list-item (“associative chaining”); others assume participants assign each item a position or order-value (“positional coding”). The field has been pointing to both kinds of models explaining behaviour, so the interesting questions are about how chaining-like versus positional-like processes trade off or combine across various task conditions. One of our tools is to ask people to recall a list backward. Strikingly, people are quite good at this task, although they think they are worse than they are. Backward serial recall largely resembles forward recall but with a small collection of very specific differences. This poses an exciting challenge to developing more complete models of serial-order memory, and to explain that metacognitive disconnect.

We also have a standing interest in memory strategies that real people voluntarily choose to use. Our procedures enable a higher level of experimental control in strategy research. Some such strategies bear an uncanny resemblance to popular formal models (e.g., the famous Method of Loci and the Peg List method both resemble positional coding, whereas the Link method is a very literal analogue of an associative chain). Computational modellers and strategy researchers appear unaware of one another’s work. Current goals are to use instructed (compliance-verified!) mnemonic strategies to inform the debate between computational models, and to use ideas from those models to understand how those same strategies actually work to support memory effectively.

Rhythmic brain activity

Our lab has a particular interest in oscillations, rhythmic brain-activity, which are increasingly thought to play important roles in cognition, including memory. We co-devised, in 2001, tested and developed an improved means of quantifying oscillations (rhythmic brain activity), now known as Better OSCillation detection (BOSC). The BOSC method is more selective for rhythmic activity than other approaches, and produces more consistent results across recording sites, tasks and even species. Tens of other labs have adopted the method and even improved upon it. We recently devised ways to make the method more robust and are continuing work to adapt it to new domains such as making it more time-adaptive and developing a continuous-valued version.

Models of associations

Just like with memory for serial lists, there are models of associations that differ mathematically, for example, whether a pair of items, when recalled, are remembered with perfect order or with no order. We found a mid-level relationship between association-memory and memory for constituent-order challenging existing models. We then adapted convolution models (which normally have no order) to satisfy these empirical constraints while not losing previous benchmarks. We plan to expand the repertoire of such models, and to begin to build associative chaining models from these, to circle back to explaining serial-order memory.
