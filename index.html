About the lab

We take a computational approach because mathematics and programming are all about specifying assumptions and processes precisely, a way to track and check our assumptions.

Research

Current active research directions

All research
  1. 01 Characteristics of feature subspaces Our attentional subsetting theory makes the models more realistic, and in doing so, is starting to capture new aspects of memory behaviour.
  2. 02 Linking brain activity to memory We develop and refine approaches at a meta-level to bring us closer to identifying, and eventually understanding, that memory-relevant brain activity.
  3. 03 Serial-order memory One of our tools is to ask people to recall a list backward.
  4. 04 Rhythmic brain activity We co-devised, in 2001, tested and developed an improved means of quantifying oscillations (rhythmic brain activity), now known as Better OSCillation detection (BOSC).
  5. 05 Models of associations We then adapted convolution models (which normally have no order) to satisfy these empirical constraints while not losing previous benchmarks.

Lab tasks

We focus on “lab tasks” such as randomly assembled lists of words, letters or digits. Participants typically study a list once and then are given a memory test for that list. We are interested in the patterns of errors people make. These tasks inherit decades or longer of the cognitive psychological tradition. This gives us a lot of experimental control and keeps the experimental work close to mathematical models.

Brain activity

With this core approach in mind, we also record brain activity (mostly electroencephalography: event-related potentials and oscillations) while people study and remember materials to discover how the brain supports those processes designed into the mathematical models. Brain activity signals include rhythmic activity and event-related potentials.

Memory strategies

We have also been exploring instructable memory strategies including interactive imagery, peg methods and the method of loci. These provide a way to interrogate basic memory processes by pushing the limits of how well, and how, people can memorize arbitrary lists.

An everyday example

For example, a friend might tell you their phone number.

You need to remember a 7- (or 10-) digit list long enough to enter it into your Contacts. That task is called immediate serial recall. We contribute to ongoing arguments about how people remember the list in order. Other ways to test memory for order include judgements of relative order (of Justin and Mark, who left the party later?) and judgements of spacing (how many people left between those two?). Models also differ in terms of how two items, like words, are paired together in memory. A particular interest for us is how order is stored within those associations.
